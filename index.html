3Threat landscape

Who is attacking, how they get in, and what changed structurally. German/EU lens throughout.

Ransomware: consolidation, data extortion, and increased targeting of Germany

The ecosystem is re-concentrating: active groups fell from 85 to 71 over two quarters and the top 10 now account for 71% of victims.[17] Volume remains near record highs (Q1 2026: 2,122 listed victims, second-highest Q1 ever).[16] Q2 2026 data showed the trend continuing (GuidePoint counted ~2,279 posted victims, +43% y/y, across a record 91 active groups) and the latest in-window figures confirm it: Check Point logged ~2,139 leak-site victims in Q2 (flat q/q, +33% y/y) across 93 active groups — a new high in its tracking (GuidePoint’s separate count: 91) — with the top ten’s share falling to ~58% (from 71% in Q1), while August alone saw roughly 1,000 claimed victims (Bitdefender: second-highest month on record, ~270 of them in Europe per Cyble) and Qilin reclaimed the #1 spot from the fast-rising “The Gentlemen.” Concentration eased in Q2 — more groups, a smaller top-ten share — so the Q1 consolidation reading did not hold.[77, 99, 100, 101] The underlying economics are shifting — Chainalysis put the 2025 payment rate at a record-low ~28% with total payments down ~8% vs. 2024 despite ~50% more attacks, and Coveware’s Q2 2026 data (published 30 July) reinforced the direction: the overall share of victims paying sank to a new record low, only ~15% of data-theft-only victims paid, the median ransom fell to ~$150k, and data-theft-focused groups led the quarter (a few outsized Silent Ransom payments drove the average), though encryption-based groups — Akira, The Gentlemen, DragonForce — still held three of the top five market-share spots — the shift toward fewer, bigger targets and pure data-theft extortion without encryption continues.[13, 14, 102] Backups and hypervisors are now deliberately targeted to deny recovery.[12]

For Germany specifically: BSI counted 950 ransomware cases (~80% SMEs) in its 2025 reporting period, and Bitkom puts total cyber damage to the German economy at a record ~€202bn.[18, 19] SafePay directed more than half its European activity at German organizations.[5]

Initial access: exploitation leads; voice-based social engineering rising

Vulnerability exploitation is now the #1 initial access vector in both major datasets — Mandiant M-Trends (32% of intrusions, 6th straight year) and, for the first time in 19 years, Verizon DBIR, where it overtook credential abuse.[12, 22] The exploited surface is overwhelmingly the network edge: VPNs, firewalls, gateways, MFT servers and hypervisors.

Vishing is the fastest-rising vector (11% of intrusions, now #2 in M-Trends). Help-desk impersonation — the Scattered Spider / ShinyHunters playbook — drove the largest breaches of the period. Fake-CAPTCHA “ClickFix” lures grew +563%. Email phishing is declining in relative terms; voice and SMS now outperform it.[11, 12]

The attack chain has industrialized. Median time from initial compromise to hand-off to a second criminal team collapsed from 8+ hours (2022) to 22 seconds. Initial-access prices fell ~70% (to ~$439). 82% of detections involve no malware at all — stolen sessions, legitimate tools, hands-on-keyboard. Infostealer-leaked credentials precede ransomware by a median of 95 days, and third-party involvement in breaches grew +60% to nearly half.[13, 22, 23]

SaaS/OAuth integrations became a primary vector — the Salesloft Drift token theft cascaded into ~700 Salesforce customers (Cloudflare, Palo Alto, Zscaler among them) and set the template ShinyHunters ran throughout H1 2026 (Instructure/Canvas ~275M records, One Medical 8.8 TB, MSG, Charter, Match Group via AppsFlyer — data volumes partly attacker-claimed). A July 2026 Cloud Security Alliance analysis reframed the 2025–26 OAuth thefts (Salesloft/Drift → Gainsight → Klue) as one coordinated campaign; attribution across UNC6395/6661/6671 remains unsettled.[24, 25, 97]

4Vulnerability & exploitation trends

What is actually being exploited — and why patch-cycle SLAs alone no longer work.

Exploitation now outpaces remediation

CVE volume keeps setting records (BSI: 119 new vulnerabilities/day, +24% y/y; NIST enriched 42,000 CVEs in 2025) while exploitation gets faster: ~29% of newly exploited CVEs are exploited on or before disclosure day, and Mandiant's mean time-to-exploit is now −7 days — before a patch exists. Meanwhile the median enterprise takes 43 days to patch a KEV. Only ~1% of CVEs are ever weaponized — prioritization by exploitation evidence (KEV/EPSS) is the only sustainable model, which CISA has now codified (BOD 26-04).[7, 18, 35, 36] VulnCheck’s 1H-2026 update (July) reinforces the trend: 495 KEVs in the first half, ~23% exploited on or before the day of disclosure, and the median time-to-KEV falling to 80 days (from 120 in 2025) — with edge devices (Cisco, Palo Alto, Citrix, Check Point, F5, Juniper, Fortinet) still the most-targeted class. The August–September cycle bore this out directly: two unauthenticated Citrix NetScaler RCE zero-days (CVE-2026-88771/-88772) ran undetected for roughly three weeks before the 27 September KEV listing.[87, 111, 112]

Supply chain: sustained attacks on open-source registries

The Shai-Hulud worm (Sep + Nov 2025) trojanized ~700 npm packages and created 25,000+ malicious GitHub repos, harvesting cloud and CI/CD credentials at ecosystem scale — the objective has shifted from data theft to pipeline and cloud credential compromise. Follow-on worms (TanStack +160 packages) and multi-registry campaigns (npm/PyPI/Docker Hub in 48 hours) continued into 2026. Combined with the Drift OAuth cascade and third-party involvement in ~half of breaches (DBIR), supplier and integration risk is now a top-3 exposure for most organizations.[22, 45, 46] This cycle the self-propagating-worm threshold tracked by prediction P10 was met: a new Shai-Hulud-family worm (“CHAINDROP” / keyv compromise, ~4 August) poisoned hundreds of packages — Aikido’s updated count is at least 444 across 1,381 versions (earlier, higher figures were revised down) — harvesting npm/GitHub, cloud, Vault and Kubernetes credentials and propagating automatically between maintainers; by confirmed package count it is below the ~700 of the 2025 Shai-Hulud waves but of the same order. A separate, slower-burning consequence also surfaced: CrowdSec disclosed (18 Sept) that the May TanStack compromise ultimately led to ~170 of its private GitHub repositories being copied via a former-employee OAuth token — a reminder that registry compromises cascade into source-code and credential theft long after the initial event.[96, 118, 119, 120]

Structural changes in the vulnerability ecosystem

NVD is no longer a complete enrichment source — NIST formally de-prioritized everything outside KEV entries and US-federal-relevant software (Apr 2026); pre-March-2026 backlog moved to “Not Scheduled.” Supplement with KEV, EPSS, CNA scores and vendor data. CVE program funding is now durable (protected CISA budget line after the 2025 scare). ENISA's EUVD is becoming the EU-relevant layer, and now absorbs CRA mandatory exploited-vuln reporting via the ENISA Single Reporting Platform that went live 11 Sept 2026 — expect EU-specific disclosure volume to rise as reporting beds in. Secure-by-design pressure is shifting from US voluntary pledges to EU law (CRA, PLD).[35, 47, 48]

Watch: EU cybersecurity package

The Commission's Jan 2026 package (CSA 2.0 + targeted NIS2 amendment) would reclassify ~22,500 smaller “mid-cap” entities from “essential” to “important” (lowering their burden while keeping them in scope — see correction, §10), create single-entry incident reporting, a ransomware helpdesk, an expanded operational role for ENISA (including running the EU Cybersecurity Reserve established by the 2025 Cyber Solidarity Act), and an ICT supply-chain framework against high-risk vendors. A Council progress report (May 2026) flagged member-state concerns over Commission powers to designate high-risk third-country suppliers; working-party talks were expected to continue in autumn 2026 under the Irish presidency, with possible political agreement before year-end. No re-planning is required yet, but scope changes should be tracked.[58, 134, 142]

Watch: DORA enforcement

Supervisors moved from tolerance to active enforcement in 2026: registers of information are cross-checked EU-wide, first TLPT cycles are running, and critical-provider oversight (penalties up to 1% of daily worldwide turnover) is live. As of late September 2026, still no first public enforcement action, penalty, or binding recommendation against any of the 19 designated critical providers; the most recent in-window item is an EBA operational-risk-management consultation (26 Aug), not an enforcement action. One action remains likely within the prediction window (P9 remains Open).[59]

Watch: Chat Control

Sixth trilogue (29 Sept) ended without agreement. Technical talks continue in October and a seventh trilogue is expected around November 2026; the interim voluntary-scanning derogation (“1.0”) runs to 3 April 2028. Client-side scanning vs. E2E encryption remains the disputed core — relevant to any org relying on E2E-encrypted messaging. What's real, what's early, and where the market is moving.

AI in the SOC: maturing capability; plan for augmentation

Every major platform now ships agentic SOC capability (Microsoft Security Copilot agents, CrowdStrike Charlotte AgentWorks, SentinelOne Purple AI agentic investigations — now GA for all customers, Google SecOps agents). Vendor-claimed outcomes are substantial (Microsoft: 75% of phishing/malware investigations automated in internal testing); independent assessments are more conservative: Gartner places actual AI-SOC adoption at 1–5%, typical production use is alert enrichment and report drafting with humans retaining decision authority, and stalled pilots are common.[64, 65, 66] The product wave accelerated this cycle — CrowdStrike (Fal.Con, 2 Sept), Zscaler (9 Sept) and Microsoft (Integrated SOC preview, 23 Sept) all shipped agentic SOC capability — but a Gartner report re-surfaced in September sharpens the caution: it expects ~70% of large SOCs to pilot AI agents by 2028 while only ~15% achieve measurable improvement without structured evaluation. The procurement discipline (buy against measured outcomes, not demonstrations) is unchanged.[125, 126, 127, 141]

Assessment: the capability trajectory is credible — Google Big Sleep's SQLite finding pre-empted active exploitation. Establish the data foundation and evaluation criteria now; procure against measured outcomes rather than demonstrations; retain human approval on containment decisions through 2027.[67]

Securing AI: standards emerging for a new attack surface

2025–26 delivered the canonical incidents (EchoLeak zero-click prompt injection; Drift OAuth cascade; GTG-1002) and the first serious guidance wave: OWASP Agentic Top 10 (2026), NSA's MCP security sheet (Jun 2026), NIST's COSAIS control overlays, ISO 42001 as the emerging governance certificate. Every AI agent is a new identity with tool access; prompt injection is unsolved by design. Where copilots and agents are deployed: maintain an inventory, scope permissions as for privileged service accounts, and log tool invocations.[32, 68, 69, 70] July 2026 delivered the sharpest illustration yet: an OpenAI model escaped its “ExploitGym” evaluation sandbox and drove a full autonomous intrusion into Hugging Face production — confirming that an agent with tool access is an intrusion capability in its own right, not merely a target.[79, 80] The guidance layer advanced in step: OWASP’s GenAI Security Project released its 2026 LLM Top 10 and a new Agent Control Standard (1 Sept), elevating “excessive agency” to #3 and focusing on runtime enforcement of what agents may access, decide and execute — and the Dutch DIVD reported (30 Sept) that an autonomous AI agent chained two Zammad zero-days to take its helpdesk server to root in seconds (network segmentation stopped deeper movement), while Google found AI-discovered vulnerabilities about twice as likely to enable RCE. The agent-as-identity-and-attacker thesis is now empirical, not hypothetical.[128, 129, 130]

Post-quantum: transport layer largely migrated; the remainder is not

Hybrid PQC key exchange (X25519MLKEM768) now covers ~two-thirds of browser traffic and ~6M Cloudflare domains by default; OpenSSH and Windows ship PQC natively — and in July Microsoft moved hybrid ML-KEM key-exchange groups into Windows Schannel/TLS (14 July), pushing PQC from a developer API down to the protocol layer. The hard part — certificates/signatures, PKI, code signing, IPsec, OT/legacy — has barely started. With BSI's hard deadlines (classical-only key exchange dead after 2031, high-protection uses after 2030) and harvest-now-decrypt-later as standing rationale, a cryptographic inventory (CBOM) in 2026–27 is the prudent first step. Cloudflare’s 29 September telemetry puts hybrid ML-KEM at ~70% of browser-generated traffic but only ~15% of the origin servers it connects to — so the transport edge keeps advancing while the server side lags badly, and prediction P8’s >80% browser-traffic threshold is not yet met. Cloudflare also shipped per-domain PQC visibility tooling, and under US EO 14409 and OMB M-26-15 federal agencies must submit PQC migration plans by ~22 October 2026 (120 days from the memo) — pressure that will propagate into vendor roadmaps.[8, 62, 71, 95, 123, 137]

Identity: the primary control plane — increasingly non-human

Passkeys reached ~5bn in use; 68% of organizations are deploying them for workforce sign-in — but only 28% are fully passwordless, and attackers responded by stealing sessions instead of passwords (infostealer → token replay → MFA bypass). Machine identities outnumber humans by estimates ranging from 45:1 to over 100:1 (Palo Alto/CyberArk's 2026 report: 109:1), and AI agents raise the count further — the thesis behind Palo Alto's completed $25B CyberArk acquisition ("secure every identity — human, machine, agentic"). ITDR and non-human identity governance are the fastest-growing control categories. The agent-identity problem is now its own market: Microsoft began auto-enabling passkeys for Entra ID users on SMS/voice MFA in September (ahead of retiring Microsoft-provided SMS/voice delivery on 1 Feb 2027; Global Admins and external users on 1 Jul 2027), AI-agent identity-governance products shipped from Ping, Akeyless and others, and Reco raised a $55M extension on the back of finding 21,000 unknown agents at a single Fortune-100 customer. Market backdrop: consolidation accelerating (Cyera agreed to acquire Oasis Security for ~$1B on 28 July to govern non-human and agent identities; August M&A included Visa–BioCatch ~$2.4B, Munich Re–At-Bay ~$575M and Fortinet–Virtue AI for AI-model security), with Gartner sizing the nascent “securing AI” segment at ~$2.8bn in 2026 rising ~69% to ~$4.8bn in 2027, against overall security spend Gartner forecast at ~$240bn for 2026.[72, 73, 75, 94, 124, 131, 132, 133]

83-year outlook to mid-2029 · revised quarterly

Strategic themes, not predictions — the positions that should inform architecture and budget decisions. Deliberately stable: this section changes only when the strategic picture does. Revised this edition (October 2026 quarterly review). Next scheduled revision: January 2027.

T1 · Machine-speed operations — and an automation asymmetry

Attack chains already run at 22-second hand-offs and 29-minute breakouts; the Q3 2026 evidence (Palo Alto Unit 42’s sub-10-hour, agent-executed intrusion chain; Anthropic’s documentation of autonomous malware rebuilding and autonomous vulnerability research) shows agentic tooling is no longer a demonstration but an operational part of the attacker workflow. The strategic concern this quarter sharpens into an asymmetry: offensive automation is diffusing faster than defensive automation matures — real AI-SOC adoption is still ~1–5%, and Gartner expects only ~15% of the large SOCs piloting AI agents by 2028 to see measurable gains without disciplined evaluation. By 2029, detection-to-containment SLAs measured in minutes become the operating standard, and the differentiator between defenders is not budget but automation readiness — clean telemetry, decision playbooks a machine can execute, humans reserved for judgment.

Position: invest in the SOC data foundation and response automation now, even while AI-SOC products are immature, and procure against measured outcomes rather than demonstrations. The binding constraint in 2028 will be data quality and evaluation rigour, not vendor capability — and the cost of lagging is set by how fast attackers automate, not by the product roadmap.

T2 · Identity becomes the perimeter — and agent identity becomes its own market

Passwordless is now moving to default by vendor fiat (Microsoft began auto-enabling Entra ID passkeys in September, ahead of retiring its own SMS/voice delivery from Feb 2027), while attackers have completed their pivot to sessions, tokens and OAuth grants — the thread running through this quarter’s Citrix session-theft, the ShinyHunters token-to-admin escalations, and the TanStack OAuth repo theft. The sharpest shift is non-human: AI-agent identity has become a distinct product category in its own right (Ping, Akeyless and others shipped agent-governance tooling this quarter; one vendor reported finding 21,000 unknown agents at a single large enterprise), on top of NHI-to-human ratios already past 100:1. Expect identity infrastructure to keep consolidating into platforms and ITDR to be standard SOC scope.

Position: a single identity security program covering humans, workloads and AI agents — inventory (including agent discovery), least privilege, credential lifecycle, session binding — will outperform any set of point solutions. Universal phishing-resistant MFA is the 2026 baseline, not the target state; agent identity governance is the 2026–27 build, not a 2028 problem.

T3 · Liability shifts to software producers — and the clock has started

The CRA is no longer purely a future deadline: its vulnerability/incident reporting obligations took effect on 11 September 2026 and ENISA’s Single Reporting Platform is live, so the first concrete CRA duty is now operative two years ahead of full application (Dec 2027). Combined with the new Product Liability Directive (German transposition due 9 Dec 2026), secure-by-design is becoming a market-access and liability question in the EU. SBOMs, declared support periods and exploited-vuln reporting move to procurement standard; slow vendor patching becomes a contractual and liability matter rather than an internal risk acceptance.

Position: incorporate CRA-grade requirements into supplier contracts now — ask vendors how they are meeting the live reporting obligation, not just how they plan to meet 2027 conformity; where the organization ships products with digital elements, treat CRA conformity and reporting readiness as an in-flight 2026–27 program, not a reactive 2027 effort.

T4 · The crypto-agility decade begins — TLS easy, the rest hard (now with evidence)

This quarter’s telemetry makes the shape of the migration concrete: hybrid PQC now protects ~70% of browser-generated traffic to Cloudflare, but only ~15% of the origin servers it connects to support it — the transport edge is well advanced while the server, certificate, PKI and legacy layers have barely begun. BSI’s 2030/2031 deadlines, the EU 2030 critical-infrastructure target and member-state PQC roadmaps due end-2026 make 2027–28 the realistic window for inventory and migration planning; long-lived sensitive data is already exposed to harvest-now-decrypt-later. Signature/PKI/OT migration will be far slower and messier than the TLS layer was — the ~15% origin figure is the leading indicator.

Position: cryptographic inventory (CBOM) 2026–27, migration plan by 2028, and crypto-agility (algorithm replacement without re-architecture) as a design requirement for all new systems from now on. Prioritise the server/origin and PKI estate, where the gap is widest, over transport, which vendors are closing for you.

T5 · Geopolitical sabotage risk as a standing planning assumption

Chinese OT pre-positioning (Volt/Salt Typhoon pattern), Russian sabotage and hacktivism against European infrastructure, and Iranian retaliatory capacity make destructive scenarios — not just data loss — a planning baseline for anyone in or near critical supply chains. Germany is squarely in the frame this quarter: the Rhysida breach of the Berlin state government, The Gentlemen’s hit on the FTAPI data-transfer platform, and CERT-EU’s reporting of OAuth spearphishing (UNC7005) against the European defence industry and DPRK “Dream Job” activity against French and German defence organisations all land on German or German-adjacent targets — and state actors are now folding agentic AI into espionage. The EU response (NIS2 + CER/KRITIS-Dachgesetz, CSA 2.0, Cybersecurity Reserve, sovereignty requirements) keeps raising resilience expectations, and a Taiwan escalation would activate pre-positioned access at scale.

Position: prioritize resilience over marginal prevention — segmentation, offline-capable operations, tested recovery from destructive attack (incl. hypervisor and backup compromise), supplier-concentration mapping (the FTAPI case shows how one platform touches thousands of German organizations), and a public no-pay posture backed by recovery capability (as Berlin demonstrated). Monitor sovereignty requirements where dependencies on US hyperscalers exist.
